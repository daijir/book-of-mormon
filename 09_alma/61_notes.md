### Alma 61:1–21 — Pahoran’s Magnanimous Response to Moroni

In the end, Pahoran wrote back to Moroni (Alma 61:1–21). His reply stands as one of the most remarkable documents in the entire Book of Mormon, providing a profound study in Christian humility, emotional poise, and statesmanship under extreme duress.

#### The Crisis in the Capital: Pachus Seizes Zarahemla

While Moroni had assumed that the central government in Zarahemla was sitting on its thrones in thoughtless stupor and luxury, Pahoran had been up to his neck in his own desperate struggle.

A faction of royalist dissenters (king-men) led by a man named **Pachus** had risen up in insurrection, allied themselves with the king of the Lamanites, and driven Pahoran out of the capital. Pahoran had fled to the neighboring city of **Gideon**, where he was attempting to rally true freemen to resist the treasonous usurpers:

> “And they have risen up in rebellion against me, and also those of my people who are freemen, yea, and those who have risen up are exceedingly numerous. And it is those who have sought to take away the judgment-seat from me who have been the cause of this great iniquity; for they have used great flattery, and they have led away the hearts of many people, which will be the cause of sore affliction among us.” (Alma 61:4–5)

#### Rejoicing in the Greatness of Moroni’s Heart

Rather than taking offense at Moroni’s stinging accusations, sharp reprimands, and threats of insurrection, Pahoran looked past the harsh words to perceive Moroni's righteous motive and love for liberty:

> “And now, in your epistle you have censured me, but it mattereth not; I am not angry, but do rejoice in the greatness of your heart. I, Pahoran, do not seek for power, save only to retain my judgment-seat, that I may preserve the rights and the liberty of my people.” (Alma 61:9)

Pahoran explains that he was not negligent; the provisions and reinforcements had been cut off by the traitorous rebels in Zarahemla. He then invites Moroni to bring a small force to Gideon, unite with his loyalists, overthrow Pachus, and liberate the supplies needed by the armies on the frontiers.

#### A Pattern for Christian Communication under Fire

The correspondence between Moroni and Pahoran provides an enduring model for human relationships and leadership:
1. **Separating Motive from Delivery**: Pahoran recognized that Moroni's anger was born of profound devotion to the suffering soldiers and the cause of Christ.
2. **Refusing to Take Offense**: In David A. Bednar's phrase, "to be offended is a choice." Pahoran chose understanding over resentment.
3. **Focusing on the Real Enemy**: Instead of engaging in a destructive war of words or a duel of egos, both leaders immediately coordinated their efforts against the true adversaries of liberty and peace.
